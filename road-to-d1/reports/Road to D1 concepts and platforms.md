# Keep Tre and Gus, aim lower

Keep Road to D1. It sits in a lane nobody else occupies, and no rival concept beats it, so sharpen it instead of replacing it. Every animated football comedy found in this research is celebrity parody built by a media company: Bleacher Report's Gridiron Heights and its 2026 college show Big College Noon Day, and 442oons in soccer. **No animated series about high-school recruiting, small-college football or long snappers turned up anywhere.** The ranked plan is: **(1) Road to D1: Offer Season** as the flagship, **(2) Gus's Snapper Diaries**, a cheap 10–20 s spin-off that satirises the real long-snapper camp-and-ranking economy, and **(3) Saturday at Hollow Creek**, a fictional D3 team whose weekly episode reacts to real D3 results from your scores site, launched for the 2027 season. Post to **all three platforms: yes, yes, yes.** Upload the same clean master file to each, keep episodes at **62–75 s** (Ep 1's 74.4 s fits), use only your own music, and add no AI label. YouTube Shorts is where the durable money is, TikTok is where discovery happens, and Instagram is for reach through shares. The one thing to change now is the series bible's safe zone. Its **22% bottom margin (422 px) is too small**: the platforms' own overlays cover roughly the bottom **35% (660–672 px)**, and its 10% top margin is also tight. The real risk is not AI labels. It is YouTube's 2025–2026 crackdown on templated faceless channels, and episodes avoid it by being clearly different from each other.

## Decisions at a glance

| | Decision | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Concept #1** | Road to D1: Offer Season (keep) | Each episode covers one recruiting ritual (camp, DM, junior day, official visit). Tre fails at it and Gus succeeds without trying. |
| **Concept #2** | Gus's Snapper Diaries (add) | 10–20 s deadpan clips with one character and one line. These are the cheap posts between episodes. |
| **Concept #3** | Saturday at Hollow Creek (fall 2027) | A fictional D3 team with a weekly episode keyed to a real D3 storyline. "Real scores: link in bio." |
| **TikTok** | **Yes**, primary for discovery | Over 60 s, or Creator Rewards pays nothing. 10k followers to qualify. Use a searchable caption. |
| **YouTube Shorts** | **Yes**, primary for money | Own music only, because any Content ID claim on a Short over 60 s blocks it worldwide. Add a series playlist and give each episode a distinct title. |
| **Instagram Reels** | **Yes**, for reach | At most 5 hashtags, a keyword-rich first caption line, and an ending people want to send to a friend. Mirror to Facebook Reels if you want Meta money. |
| **Cadence** | 2–3 episodes plus 2–3 short clips a week | Post the same file natively on every platform. Never re-upload a TikTok download that carries its watermark. |
| **AI label** | None required | Cartoon characters are exempt. Don't imitate real voices, and check that the export doesn't embed C2PA credentials. |
| **Safe zone** | **Fix the bible** | Keep important content inside x 120–880, y 290–1250. |

## Football cartoons parody stars; nobody animates the other million kids

The incumbents all work from the same template: celebrity caricatures reacting to the week's news, made by a writers' room. Bleacher Report runs Gridiron Heights (NFL), Game of Zones and The Champions ([Digiday](https://digiday.com/future-of-tv/bleacher-report-now-three-hit-animation-series)). In May 2026 it launched a B/R Cartoons YouTube channel ([Bubbleblabber](https://www.bubbleblabber.com/2026/05/when-fandoms-collide-bleacher-report-launches-dedicated-animation-channel-as-sports-and-anime-converge/?amp=1)), and through the 2026 season it posts **Big College Noon Day**, a weekly animated pregame parody about big programs ([Bleacher Report](https://bleacherreport.com/articles/25495867-watch-debut-episode-brs-new-animated-series-big-college-noon-day)). 442oons has proved that one faceless animated sports brand can scale: about **4.7M YouTube subscribers**, bought by Footballco in June 2025 ([Inside World Football](https://www.insideworldfootball.com/2025/06/04/footballco-acquires-football-funnies-animator-442oons/)). "Animated college football" is therefore no longer empty. But B/R is moving toward big-time programs, and its creative lead says, "We don't skewer or make fun of" ([Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/business/bleacher-report-br-cartoons-zach-mccann-261027.html)). That leaves the affectionate but honest, unglamorous side of the sport open.

What's missing is everything below the stars. Searches for animated series about recruiting, D2/D3/NAIA/JUCO, long snappers, kickers, linemen or walk-ons found **nothing**. The nearest neighbours are both live action: Coach Johnson Universe, a YouTube recruiting drama ([Eleven Warriors](https://www.elevenwarriors.com/forum/anything-else/2021/09/124951/cjucoach-johnson-universe-youtube-series-highly-recommended)), and Country Ball, a TV-MA streaming series ([Tubi](https://tubitv.com/series/300004059/country-ball/)). One caveat: search engines index TikTok poorly. Before you commit, search in the app for "long snapper animation", "recruiting cartoon" and "#d3football".

The potential audience is large. About **1.03 million boys play 11-player high-school football at 14,269 schools** ([NFHS 2024-25](https://a-us.storyblok.com/f/1022696/x/85a6414d04/2024-25-nfhs-high-school-athletics-participation-survey.pdf)), and each comes with parents. About 239 D3 programs carry rosters of roughly 100–111 players, because D3 has no scholarship cap ([D3football.com](https://d3football.prestosports.com/columns/around-the-nation/2014/its-size-of-fight-in-dog)). That works out to roughly **22,000–27,000 D3 players**, not the "80k+" figure sometimes quoted. These are the families who already use a live-scores site like yours.

The source of the jokes is well documented: the gap between status and reality. SI calls the non-committable offer "one of recruiting's open secrets" ([SI](https://www.si.com/high-school/recruiting/the-hidden-truth-about-college-football-offers-what-recruits-don-t-know-is-hurting-them-01kb8aj5z4sj)). A recruiting service was ordered by the New York Attorney General to repay parents ([Sports Destinations](https://www.sportsdestinations.com/management/education/sports-parents-being-targeted-fly-night-college-17518)). Kohl's ranks long snappers for every class through 2028, but only snappers its own coaches have seen at a Kohl's camp ([Kohl's](https://kohlskicking.com/national-player-ratings/long-snappers/2026)). A new Maryland snapper says his goal is to be a "ghost" at an "undervalued spot" ([SI](https://www.si.com/college/maryland/football/maryland-s-new-long-snapper-aims-to-be-ghost-at-undervalued-spot-01kgte1vfbyv)), which is Gus almost word for word.

## Three concepts ranked, and five that lost

**1. Road to D1: Offer Season** wins because it already exists and it aims at the largest audience: high-school players and their parents. The sharpening: each episode is one real recruiting ritual, with an on-screen running tally of Gus's accidental offers next to Tre's one "offer", which turns out to be a camp invite. The "Blessed to receive…" offer graphic is a stock social-media format ([SI](https://www.si.com/high-school/recruiting/the-offer-isn-t-the-finish-line-why-a-college-football-scholarship-offer-is-only-the-beginning-01kk27g3fw6c)), so use it as the target of the joke and never as your own visual style. Avoid the crowded formats: celebrity caricatures, hype edits, live-action-style POV skits, and anything that looks like faked highlights. FootballScoop reported in February 2026 that AI-faked recruit highlights are now circulating ([FootballScoop](https://footballscoop.com/2026/02/27/ai-artificial-intelligence-has-officially-spilled-over-into-highlight-videos-beware)).

**2. Gus's Snapper Diaries** is the cheapest way to post more often, and it occupies the emptiest niche. Each clip is one character, one line and 10–20 s: one snap, one camp ranking, or one coach getting his name wrong. This is also where the strongest stand-alone idea, *The Snap Count*, should live. In that world long snapping is the only sport that matters: a fictional "Snapworks National Showcase" ranks 13-year-olds, and there is a snapper draft-day show. The real version already exists, with national camps and class rankings ([ESPN](https://www.espn.com/blog/playbook/fandom/post/_/id/21533/lines-growing-long-at-long-snapping-camps)) and snappers who build their recruitment on YouTube ([Yahoo Sports](https://sports.yahoo.com/youtube-videos-helped-long-snapper-110045732.html)). Run it as a segment inside Gus's diaries first, and spin it out only if those clips beat the main show.

**3. Saturday at Hollow Creek** is the one concept nobody else *can* do, because it runs on your own data. A fictional D3 team, with a coach who is also the bus driver and a kicker who is student-body president, gets an episode every Monday that reacts to a real D3 storyline your site surfaced: a 4OT finish, a 70-point game, an upset. No real school names appear on screen. It gives the series a weekly calendar beat and a natural "real scores, link in bio" call to action. It ranks third only because of timing: the D3 season is ending, and the concept needs a full fall. Pilot it for 2027. A related variant is to time-skip Tre onto Hollow Creek's 115-man roster as one of 14 receivers. That makes a strong season-two arc for Road to D1 rather than a separate show.

The runners-up are worth keeping as ideas. **Bleacher Row** (four football parents, told only through their reactions in the stands) aims at the largest adult audience and is cheap to animate, but it puts Tre and Gus aside. **Inbox Zero** (a D3 recruiting coordinator watching 400 highlight tapes a day) and **The Big Uglies** (an offensive-line group chat) are cheap screen-based formats. **Last Chance JUCO** loses: live action already covers it, and its themes are hard to keep family-safe. None of these has been tested with an audience. The right test is 3–5 cold-open clips per concept, compared on completion rate.

## Same file, three platforms: what each one actually rewards

**Length.** 62–75 s is the right length for all three platforms, for different reasons. TikTok's Creator Rewards pays only for videos **longer than 1 minute** ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/introducing-the-new-creator-rewards-program?lang=en)). Buffer's study of 1.1M TikToks found that videos over 1 minute averaged **43% more reach** than 30–60 s ones, although that is a correlation, not a controlled test ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/longer-tiktoks-get-more-views-data/)). Shorts and Reels both allow 3 minutes ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15424877?hl=en); [Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/instagram-reels-length)). Completion rate drops steadily as videos get longer, so a 74 s episode needs a fresh beat every 5–8 s, not just a strong cold open. Viewers decide whether to swipe in the first 1–2 s ([ContentStudio](https://contentstudio.io/blog/youtube-shorts-analytics-guide)).

**Cross-posting.** Cross-posting your own episodes is safe; reposting watermarked files is not. Instagram makes Reels carrying other apps' watermarks less discoverable ([Tubefilter](https://tubefilter.com/2021/02/10/instagram-reels-with-tiktok-watermark-less-discoverable/)), but Mosseri says your own logo is fine ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-clarifies-that-including-your-own-logo-on-a-reel-is-ok/730852/)). Instagram's 2024 and April 2026 originality rules penalise accounts that mostly repost *other people's* content ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2026/04/30/new-instagram-policies-target-reposted-content/)), which doesn't describe you.

**Money.** The order is **YouTube, then TikTok, then Instagram.** On YouTube, 500 subscribers plus 3M Shorts views in 90 days unlocks fan funding, and **1,000 subscribers plus 10M Shorts views in 90 days unlocks ad-revenue share** ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13429240?hl=en)). TikTok's program requires 10k followers and 100k views in 30 days. It pays roughly **$0.40–$1.00 per 1,000 qualified views**, which means about $400–$1,000 per million views; that rate is a third-party estimate ([Influencer Marketing Hub](https://influencermarketinghub.com/tiktok-creator-rewards-program/)). Instagram has **no standing pay per Reels view**. Its last documented bonus, the $5k Breakthrough Bonus, paid out through Facebook ([Meta](https://creators.facebook.com/breakthrough-bonus/)), so mirroring episodes to Facebook Reels is the only route to Meta money. One report says YouTube will raise its Shorts thresholds in February 2027, but no official page confirms it ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/pt/blog/post/youtube-partner-program-guide)).

**Music.** Music is where your synthesized score turns out to be a real advantage. **A Short longer than 1 minute with any active Content ID claim is blocked worldwide** ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15424877?hl=en)), and YouTube's in-app library songs are capped at 30–90 s ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13486873?hl=en)). Your own music can't be claimed, unless you later distribute the theme through a service that registers it with Content ID; if you do, whitelist your own channel first. On TikTok and Instagram, your soundtrack becomes a reusable "original sound" named after the series. Vendor claims that trending sounds add 15–25% reach are unverified ([ttsvibes](https://insights.ttsvibes.com/tiktok-original-sound-vs-trending-sound-performance/)), and the extra reach isn't worth the claim risk on Shorts.

**Metadata.** Instagram has capped hashtags at **5 since December 2025** and ranks on caption keywords and on-screen text instead ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-implements-new-limits-on-hashtag-use/808309/)). Instagram's top ranking signals are watch time, likes per reach and **sends per reach** ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-shares-algorithm-insights-2025/738034/)), so "send this to your long snapper" beats "comment below". Burned-in captions remain worth it: older Facebook research found a roughly 12% gain in watch time ([3Play Media](https://www.3playmedia.com/blog/studies-find-captions-improve-engagement)).

**Cadence.** Buffer's 2025 datasets put the efficient range at **2–5 posts a week on TikTok** ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/how-often-should-you-post-on-tiktok/)) and **3–5 a week on Instagram** ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/how-often-to-post-on-instagram/)), and consistent posting matters more than daily posting. Serialised stories only work when episodes come out fast. Fruit Love Island posted about 20 episodes in two weeks and reached about **3.1M followers in 9 days** ([Tribune](https://tribune.com.pk/story/2598994/ai-generated-fruit-love-island-videos-go-viral-on-tiktok)). A weekly cliffhanger loses its pull, so every episode must make sense to a viewer who has never seen one. Expect a slow first month or two: Land of Boggs's Shorts did nothing for months before they took off ([Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2022/05/11/creators-on-the-rise-the-land-of-boggs/amp/)). With a 13-minute render, the bottleneck is writing, not computing.

## The AI label is a non-issue; looking templated is the real threat

None of the three platforms requires a label for cartoon characters voiced by TTS. YouTube requires disclosure only for content that *seems realistic*, and it explicitly exempts animation ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en-GB)). TikTok's rule also covers **realistic** AI content, and TikTok says a label doesn't affect distribution ([TikTok Support](https://support.tiktok.com/en/using-tiktok/creating-videos/ai-generated-content)). Instagram's August 2026 reach limit targets unlabeled accounts featuring **AI-generated people** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/31/instagram-puts-new-limits-on-undisclosed-ai-profiles/)), not cartoon studios. Three cautions still apply. Never imitate a real coach's or announcer's voice. Make sure no export step writes C2PA Content Credentials, because TikTok auto-applies a label it won't let you remove. And note that TikTok's November 2025 slider lets viewers turn down AI content ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/11/18/tiktok-now-lets-you-choose-how-much-ai-generated-content-you-want-to-see/)), which only matters if a label is attached.

The bigger risk is looking mass-produced. In July 2025 YouTube renamed its "repetitious content" monetization rule to **"inauthentic content"**, aimed at mass-produced videos with only superficial differences ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/youtube-clarifies-monetization-update-inauthentic-repeated-content/752892/)). A June 2026 report says the algorithm changes that followed are hitting **faceless creators who never used AI at all** ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/youtube-ai-slop-crackdown-faceless-creators-collateral-damage)); its full text wasn't read for this report. NPR names synthetic voices as one of the tells viewers use to spot slop ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2025/08/28/nx-s1-5493485/ai-slop-videos-youtube-tiktok)). A procedural pipeline with fixed voices and a fixed shot rhythm fits that profile on paper. The defence is authorship that shows: vary the set, the shot plan and the joke structure in every episode, don't reuse the same outro, and keep continuity jokes that only a writer could plant. The bible's "LOCKED" rule should cover the characters, not the episode template.

## The bible's safe zone protects the wrong band

None of the platforms publishes an official safe zone for normal posts, and third-party figures disagree. Across the cautious sources, though, the bottom **660–672 px** are covered by overlays: YouTube Shorts uses 288 px top, 672 px bottom and 192 px right, based on Google's ad specs ([Socialync](https://www.socialync.io/tools/safe-zones/youtube-shorts)), and other sources give Reels 269 px top and 672 px bottom, and TikTok 240 px top and 660 px bottom ([Upload-Post](https://www.upload-post.com/tools/safe-zone-checker/)).

| Edge | Bible (at 1080x1920) | Cautious research figure | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bottom | 22% = **422 px** (clear below y 1498) | **660–672 px** (clear below y ~1250) | **Conflict.** Anything in y 1250–1498 can sit under captions, usernames or the subscribe row. |
| Top | 10% = **192 px** | **240–290 px** | **Conflict.** It sits under the Shorts and Reels top bars. |
| Right | 14% = 269 px | 120–192 px | The bible is stricter. Keep it. |
| Left | not set | 48–120 px | Add 120 px. |

Your captions, centred at about 58% height (y ≈ 1114), already sit inside the safe band, so Episode 1's text is probably fine. What the 22% rule fails to protect is everything else placed below y 1250: on-screen offer tallies, phone and DM screens, Gus's feet in a wide shot, or a punchline prop. Change the bible to **x 120–880, y 290–1250** and redraw the `SafeZoneDebug` overlay to match. Then do the only check that really settles it: post a grid overlay privately on each app and look.

## Conclusion

The research points one way: the opening for Road to D1 is not a better joke structure but a **better-aimed audience**. Every funded competitor parodies the few thousand players at the top. You have characters built for the million below them, and a live-scores site that already reaches the families of the D3 players among them. That makes Hollow Creek more valuable to you than to anyone else, because only you can tie a cartoon to Saturday's real D3 results every week. On the platform side, procedural animation is not the danger. Treating episodes as templates is. The cheapest moves left are fixing the safe zone, varying how each episode is built, and posting fast enough for viewers to follow the story.

Three things remain unverified. All the sources were read through search excerpts, because the official help centers were blocked in this session. The TikTok eligibility numbers and the Shorts threshold change are unconfirmed. And no concept has been tested with real viewers. Check each platform's in-app eligibility screen before each milestone, and let completion rates decide among concepts #1–#3.
